# Task 2 — SaaS Conversion Analysis

**Scenario:** SaaS product converting 3% free to paid. How to grow MRR?

## I would not start by listing tactics

The easy answer is to suggest 10 things: pricing tests, chatbots, drip emails. That assumes I already know the problem. I don't. 3% is just a number. The same 3% can hide five different problems, each with a different fix.

Before I recommend anything I want to know what is actually happening behind the number.

## 5 questions I would ask first

1. **Where in the funnel does the drop happen?** Of users who sign up, how many activate (use the product the way it is meant to be used at least once)? Of those, how many hit the paywall? If 70% never activate, the fix is product. If 90% activate but don't convert, the fix is pricing or timing.

2. **Who are the 3% who DO convert?** Group them by channel, company size, and use case. If converters cluster in one place, the rest of the funnel is bringing in the wrong audience. That is an acquisition problem in disguise.

3. **Is 3% actually bad for this category?** Self-serve B2B SaaS conversion ranges from 2% to 8%. Without a benchmark for this product I can't tell if 3% is broken or normal.

4. **What do cancelled trial users say?** If we have an exit survey or support tickets in the first 7 days, that tells me more than any analytics chart. If we have neither, that is the first finding.

5. **Is the issue product or pricing?** If users hit the value moment and still don't pay, it is pricing or packaging. If users never reach the value moment, pricing is irrelevant.

## My ranked guesses

Without the data, here is what I'd expect for a generic 3% product:

**1. Activation gap (most likely).** Users sign up, get stuck in setup, never reach the value moment, leave before pricing matters. Most SaaS conversion problems are activation problems wearing a conversion costume.

**2. Wrong audience entering the trial.** Free signups attract tire-kickers. ICP-fit users convert 4 to 5 times higher. If we are getting traffic from broad SEO or viral channels, the conversion ceiling is structural.

**3. Pricing or packaging mismatch.** The cheapest plan is too generous, or the most-used feature is paywalled too hard. Plan tiers don't match how people actually use the product.

**4. Mistimed paywall.** The pay screen hits before the user has felt value, or so long after they have already churned.

I am not putting "the product is bad" on this list. Products convert at 3% for fixable reasons far more often than for existential ones.

## What I would test first

**Activation gap** is the cheapest to check and the most likely cause.

**The test:** Find the one action in the first 48 hours that best predicts paid conversion. This is data work, not a guess. Then split trial users by whether they hit that event in session 1.

If activated users convert at 12% and non-activated at 0.5%, the problem is now an activation problem. Solving that is product work, much easier than solving "conversion."

If activation is already 60%+ and activated users still convert at 4%, the problem is downstream. I move to pricing or packaging.

Either result teaches me something. That is the test I want first.

**Cost:** about 2 days of analyst time if tracking is in place. About 2 weeks if we have to build the tracking, in which case the missing instrumentation is itself the first finding worth fixing.

## What would change my mind

I would drop my activation theory if:

- Activation rate is already above 60% and drop-off is at the paywall (then it is pricing).
- Converters cluster heavily on one channel (then it is acquisition mix).
- Cancelled trial users keep raising the same objection I missed (security, integrations, trust).
- 3% turns out to be at or above category benchmark (then the answer is grow the top of the funnel, not optimize the middle).

The ranking is my best guess for a generic SaaS at 3%. The real answer is whatever the data says. My job is to design the cheapest test that tells the difference between these causes, not to commit to one before the evidence demands it.
